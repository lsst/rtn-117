\section{The LSST Camera (LSSTCam)} \label{sec:lsstcam}

The design of the LSSTCam \cite{10.71929/rubin/2571927} was developed in order to meet the strict requirements set by the ambitious goals of LSST \citep{2019ApJ...873..111I, RTN-011}, as described in \cite{2024SPIE13096E..1SR}.

% Focal plane
The LSSTCam focal plane (Figure~\ref{fig:focal-plane}) is a mosaic centrally composed of 189 thick, fully depleted charge-coupled devices (CCDs) used for science imaging, organized into 3$\times$3 rafts (described in more detail in \citet{CTN-001}).
Each raft is composed of three Readout Electronics Boards (REB) providing the power and electronics of three neighboring CCDs in a row.
The corners of the mosaic contain two CCDs for guiding and one split-wavefront CCD offset intra- and extra-focally relative to the science CCDs.

% CCDs
LSSTCam CCDs are read out in 16 channels, each with its own readout electronics and signal amplifiers.
The imaging region of each channel is 2002 pixels high and 512 pixels wide for E2V sensors and 2000 pixels high and 509 pixels wide for ITL sensors, giving a full imaging area of $4096 \times 4004$ pixels (E2V) and $4072 \times 4000$ pixels (ITL).
Charge is shifted down the columns (the \emph{parallel} direction) into a serial register, which carries each row to the amplifier (the \emph{serial} direction).
During readout, each row begins with serial prescan columns and ends with serial overscan columns, and the image ends with parallel overscan rows, so that the raw readout of every channel is 2048 rows $\times$ 576 columns for both vendors (Table~\ref{tab:amp-geometry}).

The CCD clocking pattern, or sequencer, sets the timing of every parallel transfer, serial transfer, and sampling step during readout.
In order to minimize survey overhead time, the full focal plane is read out in under 3 seconds. However, data collected during DP2
include science data taken under two different sequencers, an earlier 2\,s readout version (\texttt{2s\_v30}) and a later 3\,s readout version (\texttt{3s\_v1}), as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:calibepoch}.
After the DP2 data taking period, we switched to a slightly different sequencer (\texttt{3s\_v2}). Despite the fact that no data in DP2 were collected with the camera operating under the \texttt{3s\_v2}
sequencer, all the calibrations and metrics shown in this paper were derived with the \texttt{3s\_v2} sequencer since it shows the current on-sky performance of the LSSTCam and the current state of calibration and ISR.
We will discuss the differences between these sequencers and their impact on the camera performance in the Discussion (Section~\ref{sec:discussion:sequencer}).

\subsection{Physical Model of Photon Transport in LSSTCam CCDs} \label{sec:lsstcam:detector-model}

Instrumental signatures imprinted by the detectors and readout electronics cause each recorded pixel value to depart from the incident signal.
Removing these signatures requires a model of the full signal chain, which describes how photons are converted to charge, how that charge is transferred and amplified, and how the resulting voltage is digitized.
We built this model through electro-optical characterization on the laboratory test stand at SLAC National Accelerator Laboratory, at the summit, and on the telescope during commissioning.
\citet{2024SPIE13096E..1SR} give a detailed account of the laboratory campaigns and their results.

The model also sets the structure of the calibration and instrument signature removal (ISR) pipeline.
Each artifact enters at a specific point along the signal chain, so ISR removes them in the reverse order.
It starts with the last effect imposed, at digitization, and ends with the effects that act on the charge in the silicon.
Each step therefore relies on a physical model of the effect itself.
Figure~\ref{fig:detector-boxes} shows where each artifact enters the chain, from the detector to the REB.

We consider effects that enter the signal chain as soon as the photon enters the silicon detector, where incident photons are converted to electrons that collect in the potential wells of pixels.
During the integration of an exposure, dark current adds charge that is thermally generated rather than photo-generated \citep{2024SPIE13096E..1SR}, and the brighter-fatter effect redistributes incoming charge to neighboring pixels as the wells fill \citep{2014JInst...9C3048A, 2024PASP..136d5003B}.
Additionally, changes in dopant concentration throughout the silicon bulk cause lateral electric fields that deflect the path of electrons as they drift to the potential wells, producing a fixed pattern of astrometric distortion called ``tree rings''.
At the reddest wavelengths, mainly in the $z$- and $y$-bands, light reflects off the surfaces of the 100\,$\mu$m-thick sensors before it is absorbed, and the resulting thin-film interference produces a quasi-periodic pattern of bright and dark fringes whose amplitude and phase depend on the spectrum of the incident light \citep{2023PASP..135c4503G}.

When the integration stops, the readout process begins, and the charge packets are then shifted down the columns by parallel transfer and along the serial register toward the amplifier, where a fraction of each packet is left behind as charge transfer inefficiency (CTI) or caught in serial traps in the output register and released at a later time \citep{2021JATIS...7d48002S}.

At the output node of the detector's chip, the on-chip amplifier converts charge to voltage with gain $g_1$, and from this point the signal is carried as a voltage rather than electrons.
The signal then passes through cabling to the REB, where crosstalk couples signals between neighboring amplifiers and across the detector midline \citep{2015JInst..10C5010O}, after which the analog signal processing integrated circuit (ASPIC) applies a second, temperature-dependent gain ($g_2$).
The dual-slope integrator then samples each pixel twice, once at the reset level and once at the signal level, and outputs their difference scaled by a third gain, $g_3$ \citep{SITCOMTN-086}.
Two distinct electronic offsets are added to the signal along this chain, and we keep them separate (Figure~\ref{fig:detector-boxes}).
The clock-injected offset is an unintended signal: transitions of the parallel and serial clocks couple into the video signal near the output amplifier, adding an offset of up to $\pm10$k ADU that depends on position in the readout sequence and produces the 2D bias structure and the bias ``wave'' \citep{2026arXiv260631806M}.
Bias injection, by contrast, is a deliberate stage of the REB: after the dual-slope integrator, a fixed DC pedestal of $\sim$10--20k ADU is added so that the signal, including negative excursions from noise and the clock-injected offset, stays within the input range of the ADC.
Finally, the analog-to-digital converter (ADC) maps the analog signal to digitized counts, but it introduces non-linearities in the response at high, mid, and low signal levels \citep{RTN-055}.

The overall gain $g$ describes the net conversion from electrons to digital ADU.
Altogether, the signal processing chain can be summarized as a change of units of the image from electrons to counts.
Detector artifacts along the chain make this conversion ill-defined.
The goal of ISR is to recover the true incident signal by undoing these effects in reverse order, each in its native units.
