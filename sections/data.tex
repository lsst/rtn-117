\section{Data}\label{sec:data}
Each instrumental effect in the photon transfer chain (Figure~\ref{fig:detector-boxes}) is characterized by a dedicated set of calibration exposures, from which we build the calibration data products for each one that applied during ISR.
Calibration campaigns produce zero-second bias frames and dark exposures measure the electronic offset and dark current; pairs of flat fields taken over a wide range of illumination levels constrain the photon transfer curve, gain, non-linearity, and brighter-fatter effect; and projected spot patterns isolate crosstalk between readout channels.
The quality and reproducibility of these products depends both on the illumination hardware and on our control of the camera and calibration systems.

There are two calibration systems that will be used continuously throughout the LSST (the flat-field illuminator and the collimated beam projector).
In this section we first describe the calibration systems used to acquire these data in the dome during commissioning (\S\ref{sec:data:calsys}).

\subsubsection{Flat-Field Illuminator}\label{sec:data:calsys:flat_field_illuminator}

The Flat-Field Illuminator is designed to provide a stable, uniform, and well-characterized
illumination required to measure the absolute and relative instrumental responses of the LSST Camera.
The physical architecture of the flat-field system is driven by the fast ($f/1.2$) optical configuration
of the Simonyi Survey Telescope, which demands exceptional control over both the angular and spatial
uniformity of the calibration wavefront.

This system aims to satisfy a photometric precision of 5 mmag in repeatability and 10 mmag in spatial
uniformity across the field-of-view. The system directs light toward a
large 10.27-meter diameter Lambertian calibration screen that is tilted to 23
degrees to align precisely with the optical axis of the telescope. Because the calibration hardware must
co-rotate dynamically with the observatory dome during operations, the primary illumination
sources, environmental enclosures, and projection optics are mounted directly onto the dome structures
rather than a static mount.

To deliver both broadband and monochromatic illumination during the LSST, the system integrates two
independent illumination subsystems within a single centralized optical projector, positioned at the
geometric center of the calibration screen structure. Broadband ``whitelight'' illumination is provided
by a specialized module featuring a collection of 10 high-power LEDs selected to span the complete
$ugrizy$ filter passbands, with one LED within the $u$ passband with the other five passbands each
getting coverage from two LEDs. For monochromatic calibration, the system incorporates a tunable laser
system that provides continuous wavelength coverage from 300 to 2600 nm at a 1 kHz pulse rate. This
laser can deliver narrow tuning steps between 0.05 and 1 nm and a spectral line width under 1 nm FWHM.\textbf{Cite a paper by Parker on this}

\subsubsection{Collimated Beam Projector}\label{sec:data:calsys:cbp}

The Collimated Beam Projector (CBP) is a separate, in-dome photometric calibration instrument to
measure the system throughput of focused light to $\mathcal{O}(\text{mmag})$ precision \textbf{Cite a CBP paper}.
The CBP consists of a modified Schmidt reflector telescope fed by the tunable laser to produce a
collimated beam of known flux. By co-moving the CBP and Simonyi Survey Telescope, we are able to
place this beam anywhere on the primary mirror and have a focused spot anywhere on the focal plane.
The CBP can switch between five different masks at the focal plane, allowing for different spot
patterns to be projected onto the focal plane. \todo{cite CBP SPIE paper}

Because many instrument signatures depend on this timing, a change to the sequencer can invalidate calibration products built under the previous one.
\citet{2026arXiv260631806M} describe how the LSSTCam sequencer has evolved since the camera arrived at the summit.
The most significant on-sky change was the move to a more relaxed total focal plane readout time from 2\,s to 3\,s.
The extra time was spent on a longer per-row readout interval, while the other sequencer parameters tuned during laboratory testing were left unchanged.
This lowered the read noise of the E2V sensors by about 0.5\,e$^-$, while the ITL sensors were largely unaffected \citep{2026arXiv260631806M}.

DP2 includes science data taken under two sequencers: the original 2\,s \texttt{2s\_v30} sequencer (epoch~1) and the \texttt{3s\_v1} sequencer (epochs~2 and 3; Figure~\ref{fig:calibepoch}).
Each sequencer required its own set of calibrations.
The move to 3\,s readout changed the read noise and bias structure measured in the overscan, and it also changed the size of the crosstalk signal, so the crosstalk coefficients had to be measured again (\S\ref{sec:processing:calibration:xtalk}).
A further update, \texttt{3s\_v2}, was introduced after the DP2 data cutoff on January 6, 2026.\todo{Briefly state what changed between \texttt{3s\_v1} and \texttt{3s\_v2}.}
Throughout DP2, detector~101 (R23\_S02) showed signal-dependent correlated noise.
This noise interfered with the photon transfer curve calibration (\S\ref{sec:processing:calibration:ptc}) and with the measurement of both gain and read noise for that detector.
The correlated noise disappeared with the move to the \texttt{3s\_v2} sequencer.
No \texttt{3s\_v2} data or calibrations are included in the DP2 release, but \texttt{3s\_v2} is the sequencer used for survey operations from now on.
For that reason, the calibration products shown in this paper are the \texttt{3s\_v2} products.
The algorithms are the same for all sequencers; only the input exposures and the resulting calibration products differ.
